## The hook

> Every marketing transaction is two questions. Why this, and then why you. Most businesses only answer the second.

---

## The core idea

Before anyone buys from you, they answer two questions in order. Why this: do I even need this kind of thing at all. Why you: of all the options, why yours. Almost everyone pours their effort into "why you," the comparison fight, while ignoring "why this," the demand question, and then wonders why the audience that's ready to compare is so small.

---

## The tension

Demand versus competition. "Why you" is the comparison game: features, price, proof against rivals. It feels like the real work, so that's where the budget goes.

But "why you" only matters to the handful of people who already answered "why this." Everyone else hasn't decided they need the category yet. Sell "why you" to them and you're arguing about brands of umbrella with someone who doesn't believe it's going to rain.

---

## Why it matters

Most of your market is stuck on the first question, not the second. They're not choosing between you and a competitor. They're not choosing at all yet. Answer "why this" and you grow the pool of people who are even in the market, instead of knife-fighting for the sliver who already are.

It also explains why so much marketing feels expensive. Skip "why this" and you only ever talk to in-market buyers, the most competitive, most expensive audience there is. Answer it and you start building demand upstream, where it's cheaper and where you get remembered before the comparison even starts.

---

## Building blocks

1. **Two questions, one order.** Why this comes first. Why you comes second. Reverse them and you're pitching your advantages to someone who hasn't agreed the problem is worth solving.

2. **"Why this" is a demand problem.** It's the job of making someone believe the category matters: that the problem is real, costly, and worth acting on now. This is the work most businesses skip entirely, then blame the leads.

3. **"Why you" is a positioning problem.** Once they want the thing, this is where your mechanism, your promise, and your proof do their work. It's necessary. It's just second, and it's only as big as the demand you built ahead of it.

4. **Most of the market is on question one.** The crowd actively comparing vendors is small. The crowd that hasn't decided they need it is everyone else. Awareness is how you reach them early, so when they finally ask "why you," your name is already in the room.

5. **Every activity is awareness.** Every post, every page, every answer you give is building the "why this." You're not just chasing this quarter's buyers. You're teaching the rest of the market that the problem exists, so they think of you when it becomes their turn.

---

## The pushback

- **"My competitors are the problem, so 'why you' is what matters."** Only inside the small group already shopping. If your pipeline feels thin, it's usually because too few people ever reached the comparison. Grow "why this" and the comparison pool grows with it.
- **"Building demand sounds slow and unmeasurable."** It's slower than chasing in-market leads, and it compounds. The "why you" fight resets every quarter. The "why this" you build keeps paying out as new buyers cross into the market already knowing you.
- **"Isn't 'why this' just educating my competitors' customers too?"** Some. But the one who answered "why this" in your words, on your post, tends to bring your name into the room when they finally ask "why you." Teaching the market is rarely charity.
- **"My category is obvious. Everyone knows they need it."** Then your "why this" is "why now," and it's the same move. Make the cost of waiting real, and you've answered the first question in a category where everyone thought it was settled.

## Where it ships

*The same idea, cut for each channel.*

### Short post (TikTok / X caption voice)
lowercase, plainspoken:

> every sale answers two questions in order. why this, then why you. why this: do i even need this kind of thing. why you: why yours over the others. almost everyone fights the "why you" battle and ignores "why this." but "why you" only matters to the people who already answered "why this." grow the first one and the second one gets a lot easier.

### Long-form (essay / newsletter)
Working title: **The Two Questions Every Buyer Asks.** Open on the order and why it matters. Separate the demand question from the positioning question. Make the case that most of the market is stuck on "why this," which is exactly the part most marketing skips. Tie it to why lead costs feel high. Close on awareness as the long game: every activity builds the why-this, so your name is there when they finally ask why-you.

### Video / talk
Cold open: "Every sale answers two questions, in order. Why this, then why you. You're only answering one of them." Three beats: the order, why-this is demand and why-you is positioning, most of the market is on question one. Last line they repeat: "answer both, and you'll sell a lot."

- Slide 2: Why this? (Do I need this kind of thing at all?)

- Slide 4: Almost everyone only answers "why you."
- Slide 5: But "why you" only matters to people who answered "why this."

## Where it leaves you

Look at your last ten pieces of marketing and sort them into "why this" and "why you." If they're nearly all "why you," you've been talking to the small crowd that was already shopping and ignoring everyone upstream. The next move is to build the case for the problem itself, so the market arrives at the comparison already knowing your name.
