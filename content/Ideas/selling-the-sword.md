## The hook

> You are not the hero of your customer's story. You're the sword they swing. Most marketing casts the brand as the hero, and that's why it doesn't connect.

---

## The core idea

Every good story has four pieces: a hero, a dragon, a reward, and a sword. Your customer is the hero. Their problem is the dragon. The life they want is the reward. Your product is the sword, the thing they pick up to win the fight. Most businesses write themselves as the hero, which leaves the customer watching someone else's adventure instead of starring in their own.

---

## The tension

Main character versus item vendor. Every instinct says be the hero: look impressive, lead with your story, your awards, your journey. Make the customer admire you.

But admiration isn't the goal. Action is. You are not the main character. You're the item vendor in the town, the one they buy the sword from before they go fight the dragon. They do the fighting. They feel the win. The moment your marketing makes the story about you, the customer stops seeing themselves in it, and a story they're not in is a story they don't act on.

---

## Why it matters

People take action inside stories where they're the hero. Put the customer in that seat and your product stops being a thing to evaluate and becomes the tool that gets them to the reward. That's the difference between a brochure and a quest.

It also fixes the most common copy mistake on the planet: talking about yourself. When the brand is the hero, every sentence is "we, we, we." When the customer is the hero, the sentences turn outward, and the customer finally hears their own problem named.

---

## Building blocks

1. **The four elements.** Hero, dragon, reward, sword. Name them for your business. If you can't say who the hero is, what the dragon is, what the reward looks like, and how your product is the sword, you don't have a story yet. You have a description.

2. **The customer is the hero. Always.** Not your founder. Not your brand. The person with the problem. Cast anyone else in that seat and the customer becomes a spectator, and spectators don't buy.

3. **Your product is the sword, not the savior.** The sword doesn't slay the dragon. The hero does, holding the sword. Your job is to be the best possible weapon for the fight they're already in, then get out of the way and let them win.

4. **The dragon is their problem, named honestly.** A vague dragon makes a weak story. The sharper and more specific the problem you name, the more the hero feels seen, and the more they want the sword that was clearly forged for that exact fight.

5. **It rewrites your whole page.** Run any "about us" paragraph through the frame. Every line about your greatness becomes a line about their journey. You stop being the impressive stranger and become the shop they can't leave town without visiting.

## The pushback

- **"But my brand story is genuinely good."** Keep it, and put it in service of theirs. Your origin matters as proof you forge a good sword, not as the main plot. The hero seat stays the customer's.
- **"Customers want to know who they're buying from."** They do, and trust still matters. But trust is the shopkeeper being credible, not the shopkeeper hijacking the quest. Be the trusted vendor, not the protagonist.
- **"This is just the hero's journey, repackaged."** It is the hero's journey, aimed at one practical mistake: brands casting themselves in the wrong role. The fix is simple and almost nobody does it.
- **"My product really is the impressive part."** To you. To the customer, the impressive part is their own transformation. Make the sword legendary, sure, but legendary because of what it lets the hero do.

## Where it leaves you

Open your homepage and count how many sentences are about you versus about them. If "we" outnumbers "you," your customer is watching your story instead of starring in theirs. The next move is to recast the page: name their dragon, picture their reward, and make your product the sword they reach for.
