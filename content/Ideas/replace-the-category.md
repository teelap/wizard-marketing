## The hook

> Stop improving the old way. Replace it. Improvement makes the customer compare. A new opportunity makes them convert.

---

## The core idea

The New Opportunity is the last domino, and it doesn't compete on the existing terms. It says the old way is fundamentally broken, and here is a different vehicle to the same destination. Improvement asks the customer to weigh you against the incumbent. A new opportunity removes the incumbent from the comparison entirely, and puts you in a category of one.

---

## The tension

Better versus different, and which one actually moves people. Better feels like the safe play: be faster, cheaper, nicer than the leader. So you position as an improvement.

That's the trap. An improvement accepts the customer's existing frame, and the existing frame belongs to the market leader. Compete on their terms and you trigger evaluation, spreadsheets, "let me compare options." Offer a genuinely new vehicle and you trigger something else. Excitement. The customer stops comparing and starts wanting.

---

## Why it matters

When you offer an improvement, the customer evaluates. When you offer a new opportunity, the customer converts, because there's nothing to line you up against. Dollar Shave Club didn't make a better razor. They built a different vehicle: a dollar a month, shipped to your door, against a thirty-billion-dollar industry that locked blades behind plastic. A $4,500 video. Twelve thousand orders in forty-eight hours. Half the online market within three years. A billion-dollar sale to Unilever, who didn't pay for razor blades. They paid for the position in 3.2 million customers' minds.

And the customer doesn't feel sold to. They feel found. Like someone finally named the thing they didn't know they were looking for.

---

## Building blocks

1. **Improvement compares. New opportunity replaces.** "20% cheaper than the leader" keeps the leader as the reference point. "The old way is broken, here's a different one" erases the reference point. One triggers evaluation. The other triggers excitement.

2. **The improvement trap is a death sentence.** The moment you position as slightly better, you've accepted the customer's existing frame, and that frame was built by the incumbent. You can't out-incumbent the incumbent. You have to change what the customer is choosing between.

3. **Deposition the old way.** Whatever you're uniquely best at, make that the game. If you're the only car with seat belts, your message becomes "shouldn't a car protect the people inside it?" You're not attacking competitors. You're attacking the paradigm, and leaving anyone still in it looking like they missed something fundamental. (Credit to Marty Marion, who put a name to this.)

4. **Make the old way unacceptable, not just slightly worse.** The test: does your positioning make the old way feel broken, or just a little behind? If it's the second, you're still improving, not replacing. The new opportunity has to make standing still feel like a mistake.

5. **It's about their liberation, not your innovation.** Done right, the new opportunity feels like a gift, not a pitch. The customer thinks "where has this been my whole life," not "let me weigh the features." If they can see the seams, you're still selling. If the seams are invisible, you're converting.

## The pushback

- **"My product genuinely is an improvement, not a revolution."** Then find the frame where it's a replacement. Agile SEO wasn't a new science. It was the old work, reorganized into weekly sprints, positioned against the static calendar. Same craft, new vehicle, and prospects couldn't go back.
- **"Isn't 'the old way is broken' just trash-talking competitors?"** No. You attack the paradigm, not the people. You don't say the leader is bad. You say the whole category's assumptions are outdated, which makes everyone still operating on them, including you-if-you-stayed, look behind.
- **"This sounds like it only works for startups."** It's how the landscaper escaped, too. They stopped competing with landscapers and sold outdoor-living transformation. A category of one, with a waiting list and premium pricing. Replacement isn't about being new. It's about renaming the game.
- **"What if customers don't want a new category, just a known quantity?"** Status quo bias is real, which is exactly why improvement loses: it keeps them comparing inside the safe old frame. A new opportunity gives them permission to leave it without feeling reckless.

## Where it ships

*The same idea, cut for each channel.*

### Short post (TikTok / X caption voice)
lowercase, plainspoken:

> stop trying to be a better version of the market leader. that just makes people compare you to them. dollar shave club didn't make a better razor. they built a different thing: a dollar a month, shipped to your door, against a $30 billion industry. a $4,500 video. 12,000 orders in 48 hours. don't improve the old way. replace it.

### Long-form (essay / newsletter)
Working title: **Stop Improving the Old Way. Replace It.** Open on the improvement trap and why "better" keeps you trapped in the leader's frame. Separate evaluation from conversion. Tell Dollar Shave Club end to end, then depositioning with the seat-belt line. Bring in Agile SEO and the landscaper's category-of-one as proof it isn't just for startups. Close on the capstone sentence: once they understand your mechanism and your outcome, the objections stop mattering. That's the whole chain falling.

### Video / talk
Cold open: "Dollar Shave Club didn't build a better razor. They built a different vehicle, and sold it to Unilever for a billion dollars." Three beats: improvement compares and new opportunity replaces, deposition the old way, make standing still feel like a mistake. Last line they repeat: "don't improve the old way. replace it."

- Slide 1: Stop improving the old way. Replace it.
- Slide 2: Improvement makes them compare. A new opportunity makes them convert.
- Slide 3: Dollar Shave Club: $1/month vs a $30B razor industry.

## Where it leaves you

Write down the one assumption everyone in your category treats as a law of nature. That assumption is your opening. The next move is to ask whether your marketing improves on the old way or replaces it, because if the old way still feels merely a little worse, you're still competing on the incumbent's terms, and the incumbent always wins those.
