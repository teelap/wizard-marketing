## The hook

> Anyone can get likes, hearts, shares, views, and clicks. Content that doesn't convert is noise.

---

## The core idea

The dashboard most businesses watch is a comfort blanket. Likes, followers, impressions, all climbing, all feeling like progress, none of them tied to money. The metrics that matter are the ones that survive the question "did the needle move." If the answer is no, the activity wasn't valuable, no matter how good the engagement chart looked.

---

## The tension

Vanity versus revenue. Vanity metrics go up and to the right, they're easy to grow, and they feel like success. So they're the ones that get reported and celebrated.

But anyone can get likes. They're the cheapest signal there is, which is exactly why they're a comfort blanket and not a strategy. Revenue metrics are harder, slower, and less flattering, and they're the only ones that tell you whether any of it worked. The easy number and the true number are rarely the same number.

---

## Why it matters

Chase engagement and you optimize for engagement, which is a trap, because engagement that doesn't convert is just an expensive way to feel busy. One conversion is worth more than a thousand clicks. A post that goes viral and sells nothing is noise with good production values.

Tie your reporting to money and the whole machine changes. You start asking which channel actually turns into revenue, and you can put a number on it. Even the industry is moving this way, with most brands now watching engagement quality over raw follower counts. The direction is clear: away from the flattering number, toward the one that pays the bills.

---

## Building blocks

1. **Anyone can get likes.** Hearts, shares, views, clicks: all cheap, all easy, all uncorrelated with whether anyone bought. The fact that the chart is going up tells you almost nothing about whether the business is. Vanity is the metric that flatters without informing.

2. **Content that doesn't convert is noise.** A viral post that sells nothing isn't a win you can spend. The question isn't "did it get attention." It's "did the attention turn into anything." If the needle didn't move, was there really value?

3. **Track what turns into money.** Opportunities first, the number of real chances to sell. Then cost per lead, measured against customer lifetime value, with healthy usually landing in the single-digit-to-low-double-digit percent range. Those numbers tell you the truth the like count won't.

4. **ROEMI: the one number that grades the whole thing.** Return on marketing investment. Gross profit, minus marketing cost, divided by marketing cost, times 100. Run it per channel and you find out which ones are actually profitable, so you can pour into those and stop funding the ones that only produce applause.

5. **Trade the comfort blanket for measurable growth.** No more smoke, no more mirrors, no more vanity reports. A rough number tied to revenue that you actually watch beats a beautiful engagement dashboard that never once told you whether you made money.

## The pushback

- **"Awareness has real value, though."** It does, when it's built on purpose and leads somewhere. The problem isn't awareness. It's mistaking a like for awareness and awareness for revenue. Build awareness deliberately and measure whether it ever converts, instead of assuming the like count proves it.
- **"Some of my best content isn't directly trackable."** Fair, and brand effects are real and lagged. But "hard to track" is not the same as "don't bother." Track what you can to revenue, and be honest about the rest instead of hiding it behind a vanity chart.
- **"My boss wants to see follower growth."** Then show follower growth next to revenue, and let the gap make the argument for you. The point isn't to ban the vanity number. It's to stop letting it stand in for the one that matters.
- **"Isn't ROEMI just ROI with a new name?"** It's ROI aimed specifically at marketing spend, run per channel, so you can see which channels actually pay. The name matters less than the habit: grade the spend by what comes back, not by what gets liked.

## Where it leaves you

Open your marketing dashboard and circle every metric that's tied to money. If most of the screen is impressions, likes, and followers, you've been grading yourself on the easy test. The next move is to add the hard numbers, opportunities, cost per lead, return per channel, and start making decisions off those instead of off the applause.
