## The hook

> A page can look perfect to a human and be unreadable to Google. Structure and schema are how you translate it.

---

## The core idea

Content is king, but a crawler doesn't read your page the way a person does. It reads structure: one clean H1, a logical header hierarchy, schema markup, and stacked, provable facts. Get the technical layer right and you're handing Google a clean translation of what your page is about, instead of hoping it figures the page out on its own. And the same legibility is now what gets you pulled into AI answers.

---

## The tension

Pretty versus parseable. A page can look beautiful, read well, and still confuse a search bot, because the bot isn't admiring the design. It's parsing the markup.

Content is king, and you still have to nail it. But great content with no structure is a brilliant argument handed over in the wrong language. The technical layer isn't decoration on top of good writing. It's the translation that gives good writing a chance to rank at all.

---

## Why it matters

Structure tells Google what matters and how it connects. One H1 says "this is what the page is about." A clean hierarchy says "here's how the sub-ideas nest." Schema says it in a language Google reads more reliably than your prose. Skip all that and you've made the crawler guess, and guessing crawlers rank you lower.

It also future-proofs you. The AI answer engines pull from structured, fact-dense, well-marked-up pages, and they reward being cited. Provable facts, clean markup, and a page built to be quoted are how you become a name the model reaches for, instead of a page it skips. (More on the owned-data side of that in rent-the-trick-own-the-truth.)

## Building blocks

1. **One H1, and never skip a gap.** Your H1 is the biggest concept on the page, with your main keyword in it, and there's only one. Below it, H2s guide the reader, H3s nest under H2s. Picture a sideways pyramid: H1, H2, H3, H2, H3, H4, H3. Never jump from H1 to H3. Skipping levels confuses the crawler.

2. **Stack provable facts.** Google wants to see facts, so add them. And it stops understanding a wall of text after about thirty to fifty words, so break content into short paragraphs under clear headers. Density plus structure reads as authority. A gray wall reads as noise.

3. **Schema is the part Google reads best.** Go to schema.org, find your type, local business and FAQ page are the workhorses, and add the markup to your page. Google reads that structured data far better than it reads your visible text. It's the cleanest way to hand over your facts in a format the machine trusts.

4. **Build an entity map.** Think about the related people, places, and things your page should mention, and include them. The more related entities you connect, the better the page performs, because you're showing Google how your topic fits the wider web of meaning, not just repeating a keyword.

5. **It's the same move for the AI answer.** Structured, fact-dense, well-marked-up pages are exactly what the AI engines quote. Clear answers under question-shaped headers, real statistics, clean schema: that's how you become a noun in the AI's mind instead of a page it scrolls past. The technical layer now serves two readers at once.

## The pushback

- **"My content is great. Isn't that enough?"** Great content is necessary and not sufficient. If the structure is a mess, the crawler can't tell what's great about it. You wrote the right answer in a format the reader can't parse.
- **"Schema sounds like developer stuff I can't touch."** It's more approachable than it looks. You can generate valid schema by describing your business to an AI, then drop it in the page header. The barrier is knowing it matters, not the difficulty.
- **"Header tags are just formatting."** To a human, mostly. To a crawler, they're the outline of your whole page. One H1 and a clean hierarchy is the difference between handing Google a table of contents and handing it a shuffled stack of paper.
- **"Isn't technical SEO a rabbit hole?"** It can be. This isn't the rabbit hole. One H1, clean headers, schema, real facts, related entities. Five fundamentals that most pages still get wrong, before any advanced anything.

## Where it leaves you

Open your most important page and check three things: how many H1s it has, whether the headers skip levels, and whether there's any schema at all. Most pages fail at least one. The next move is to fix the structure first, the part the crawler actually reads, before you touch another word of the content sitting on top of it.
