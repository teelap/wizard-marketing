## The hook

> Never design your logo until you've done three things first. The logo is the last decision, not the first.

---

## The core idea

Most businesses start a rebrand by hiring someone to draw a logo. That's the last step, dressed up as the first. Before any visual, you need three things: your brand doctrine, your brand messaging, and your brand positioning. Get those right and the logo almost designs itself. Skip them and you've bought an expensive picture sitting on top of nothing.

---

## The tension

The logo versus the foundation. A logo is fun, visible, and feels like progress, so it's where people start. You can show it to your spouse. You can put it on a hat.

But a logo is an expression of a brand, not the brand itself. Branding is colors, styles, a whole vibe, and underneath all of that, a set of beliefs. Start with the picture and you're decorating a house with no frame. The fun part has to come last, because it's the part that depends on everything else being decided.

---

## Why it matters

When the foundation is set, every downstream decision gets easier, including the logo, the colors, the copy, the hire. You stop debating taste and start expressing a position. That's the difference between a brand and a nice-looking business.

And it's where marketing actually comes from. Branding isn't marketing, but marketing comes out of branding. Nail the doctrine, the messaging, and the positioning, and your campaigns write themselves from a clear point of view instead of being invented fresh, off vibes, every quarter.

---

## Building blocks

1. **Brand doctrine: your core beliefs.** The handful of convictions that drive every decision inside the business. Apple and innovation. Nike and empowerment. Coca-Cola and togetherness. When you know your doctrine, every other call gets easier, because you have something to measure it against.

2. **Brand messaging: how the doctrine reaches the market.** This is how you communicate the doctrine distinctly, and it isn't just words. It's emotions, feelings, even colors. Messaging is the doctrine made legible to people who will never read your mission statement.

3. **Brand positioning: where you can and can't compete.** An honest map of the ground you can win and the ground you should never set foot on. Positioning gives your doctrine its edges, and edges are what make a brand sharp instead of generic.

4. **Combine the three and you become unignorable.** Doctrine plus messaging plus positioning is your brand identity. That's the thing the logo is supposed to express. Build it first and the logo has a job. Skip it and the logo is just a shape.

5. **Then, and only then, the logo.** With the foundation set, the visual decisions stop being arguments about taste. They become questions of fit. Does this express the doctrine, carry the messaging, hold the position. The logo gets easy because the hard part is already done.

## The pushback

- **"We need a logo to look legitimate now."** You need a clear point of view to look legitimate. A polished logo on a confused brand reads as a costume. Spend the foundation time first and the logo you eventually buy will actually mean something.
- **"This sounds like expensive agency theater."** It's the opposite of theater. Doctrine, messaging, and positioning are three plain documents you could draft this week. They cost thought, not budget, and they're what makes every later dollar land.
- **"Big brands change logos all the time."** They change the expression because the foundation underneath is rock solid. They're updating the picture, not discovering who they are. You're trying to skip straight to the picture.
- **"I already have a logo I like."** Fine. Then this is a check, not a redo. Hold your logo up against your doctrine, messaging, and positioning. If it doesn't express them, the logo isn't wrong, it's just early.

## Where it leaves you

If your last branding project started with a logo, you started at the end. The next move is to write the three documents nobody made you write: what you believe, how you say it, and where you compete. Once those exist, every visual decision stops being a matter of taste and becomes a matter of fit.
