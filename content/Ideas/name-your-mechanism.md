## The hook

> Almost nobody has a unique product. What you can have is a uniquely articulated process. Name it, and competitors can't copy it without copying you.

---

## The core idea

Your Mechanism is the named, sequenced, ownable system that makes your promise believable. It's what turns "we'll get you there" into "here's exactly how, and we're the only ones who do it this way." Most businesses deliver a real process every day and never name it, so the customer hears a generic claim instead of a credible system, and generic language always loses to a named one.

---

## The tension

Talent versus a system, and which one the market actually trusts. You've got real skill, so you sell the skill, freestyle, every call a little different.

But claims generate skepticism. Systems generate trust. An unnamed process feels like a promise you're hoping to keep. A named one feels like a machine that already works. You can have the better talent and still lose to the competitor who named theirs, because the market chose the thing it could understand and repeat.

---

## Why it matters

A named mechanism does two jobs at once. It makes you believable now, and it makes you unrepeatable later. Once you own the language, a competitor who uses your process has to either rename it, which is hard, or echo it, which makes them look like the copy. The territory in the customer's mind is occupied.

And the customer becomes your salesperson. When someone tells a friend "it uses Muscle Confusion" or "I'm doing the Baby Steps," they're building your moat for you, in a room you're not even in. That's a named system working without the creator present.

---

## Building blocks

1. **A name, a logic, a sequence.** That's the whole recipe. What it's called, why it works, and the order it runs in. Have all three and you've turned a service into a system. Miss the name and you've got an operations manual with no cover.

2. **Generic language always loses to a named system. Always.** "We do a thorough onboarding" is a sentence the customer forgets before they finish reading it. "The One Week Launch" is a thing they remember and ask for. Same work. One of them is ownable.

3. **The barbecue test.** Could a customer mention your mechanism at a barbecue and have the other person get it, with no follow-up question? "Muscle Confusion" passes. "Activation Protocol" does not. If it needs a footnote, it isn't named yet.

4. **A mediocre name beats no name, every time.** People wait for the perfect title and ship nothing. Don't. A mediocre name can still be owned, remembered, and repeated. No name can't. Tony Horton took periodization, a method known since the 1960s, called it Muscle Confusion, and sold over four million copies and half a billion dollars of it.

5. **You probably already have one. It's just unnamed.** The paving crew's five steps. The agency's four stages. Those aren't commodities. They're unnamed mechanisms waiting for language. Map the real journey, name each stage, name the system, and watch a freelancer turn into a method.

## The pushback

- **"Naming my process feels gimmicky."** Gimmicky is a name with no system behind it. This is the opposite: a real, repeatable process that finally has a handle the customer can grab. The work is real. You're just letting them hold it.
- **"My competitors will steal the name."** Let them. The day they use your language is the day they advertise that you set the standard. The moat isn't the name. It's being first to own it in the customer's head.
- **"Periodization already existed. Isn't Muscle Confusion just rebranding?"** Yes, and that's the point. Horton admitted it was the same concept. The market didn't pay half a billion dollars for a new science. It paid for a name it could understand and repeat.
- **"What if my process isn't actually that different?"** Then your articulation is the difference. Two firms can run the same five steps; the one that named them wins, because the customer can only trust what they can describe.

## Where it leaves you

Write down the steps you actually walk a client through, then ask whether any of them have a name a customer could repeat. If not, you're a feature list competing on price, and you didn't have to be. The next move is small and permanent: name the system you already run, and start saying it out loud until your customers say it back.
