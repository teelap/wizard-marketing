A named process outsells a better one. Not because the name does the work, but because a customer can only trust what they can repeat, and generic language hands them nothing to hold.

I have spent 13 years watching that decide deals, through two exits and a lot of pitches that should have gone the other way. The better operator loses to the clearer one more often than anybody in this industry wants to admit.

## Why a named process beats a better process

Claims generate skepticism. Systems generate trust.

That is the whole idea in two sentences. When you say "we run a thorough onboarding," the customer hears a claim, and claims are what every competitor is also making. When you say "we run the One Week Launch," the customer hears a thing. Things can be asked about. Things can be repeated. Claims just evaporate.

An unnamed process is a promise you are hoping to keep. A named one is a machine that already works. Same labor, same people, same calendar. One of them is ownable.

So you can have the better talent and still lose, because the market does not pick the best process. It picks the one it can describe.

## What actually counts as a mechanism

A name, a logic, and a sequence. All three, or you do not have one yet.

| The part | The question it answers | What you have without it |
|---|---|---|
| The name | What is it called? | An operations manual with no cover |
| The logic | Why does it work? | A slogan |
| The sequence | What order does it run in? | A list of services |

Most businesses have two of the three and never notice. The paving crew runs five steps in a fixed order and has excellent reasons for each one. They just call it "our process," which is what everyone calls theirs. The sequence is real. The language is generic. The customer files them next to the other three bids.

## The most valuable two words in fitness were not new science

Tony Horton did not invent muscle confusion. He named it.

The training principle underneath P90X is periodization, and periodization has a long, documented lineage. Its theoretical foundations were laid in the Soviet Union through the 1950s and 1960s by Lev Matveyev, then refined by Tudor Bompa working with Romanian Olympic athletes through the 1960s and 1970s. Bompa's *Theory and Methodology of Training* put it in front of English-speaking coaches. Wikipedia's own description of P90X says the program uses cross-training and periodization.

In 2005, Horton trademarked the phrase "muscle confusion." P90X went on to sell roughly five million copies.

Bompa got the science. Horton got the market. What separated them was two words and a trademark.

Read that as cynicism if you want, but the honest version is more useful: the method was not new, and the language was. Horton did not steal a result. He gave a legitimate existing method a handle a customer could hold at a dinner party, and the market paid for the handle, because the handle was the part it could actually use.

## The barbecue test

If a customer can mention your mechanism at a barbecue and the other person gets it with no follow-up question, it is named. If it needs a footnote, it is not.

"Muscle Confusion" passes. Someone says it, the other person nods, the conversation keeps moving. "Proprietary Activation Protocol" fails, because now somebody has to explain, and nobody explains your business for you at a barbecue. They change the subject instead.

That is the part people miss when they worry about sounding gimmicky. A named mechanism is not marketing you do at the customer. It is marketing the customer can do for you, in rooms you will never be in. When someone tells a friend "it uses Muscle Confusion," they are building your moat while you are asleep.

Which is why a mediocre name beats no name every single time. People hold out for the perfect title and ship nothing for two years. A mediocre name can still be owned, remembered, and repeated. No name cannot do any of the three.

## You already run a mechanism. You just never named it.

The sequence exists. The language does not. That is the real gap for almost every business reading this.

I ran the eight dominoes on myself before I ran them on a client. That framework is not a metaphor invented for a book cover, it is the order I already worked in, written down and named so other people could follow it. Same with Share of Brand Voice: the measurement lived in how I evaluated accounts long before it had a label worth repeating. Naming them did not improve the work by a single percentage point. It changed what people could say about the work when I was not in the room.

Mechanism is Domino 05 for exactly that reason. It sits after the promise, because a promise the customer believes is a promise backed by a system they can see. Make the [sixty-second promise](/grimoire/the-sixty-second-promise) first, then hand them the machine that keeps it. Out of order, a named mechanism is a clever label on an offer nobody wanted, which is its own failure and a more expensive one.

Almost nobody has a truly unique product. What you can have is a uniquely articulated process.

There is no spell here. Write down the steps you actually walk a client through, in order. Ask whether a single one of them has a name a customer could repeat. If the answer is no, you have been competing as a feature list on price, and you did not have to be. Name the sequence you already run, then say it out loud until your customers say it back to you. That is [the whole domino](/eight-dominoes).
