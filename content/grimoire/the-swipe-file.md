Don't write your first Google Ads from a blank page. Search like a customer, read the ads at the top, and write down everything in them. The ads still running are the ones that work, because nobody keeps paying for an ad that loses. Your competitors already paid for that lesson. Collect it for free.

I've spent 13 years in marketing, including an agency that crossed seven figures running SEO and paid ads. The most expensive habit I saw in new ad accounts was originality. An owner would write three clever headlines, launch, and pay to learn what the market already knew. The cheapest research in marketing sits on the search results page. Almost nobody reads it.

## What is a swipe file?

A swipe file is a collection of ads that already worked, saved so you can learn from them before you write your own. The word "swipe" is the joke. You aren't stealing the ad. You're stealing the lesson.

For Google Ads, the swipe file builds itself. Every search your customer runs shows you a handful of ads from businesses competing for that exact click. Those ads are a live, market-tested research file, and they are free to read.

## How do you build a Google Ads swipe file?

You build it in four steps, and it takes about twenty minutes.

1. **Go to Google.** Open a private window so your own history doesn't shape what you see.
2. **Pretend you're a customer.** Type in what you want to buy, the way a buyer would say it. "Emergency plumber near me." "Bookkeeping for contractors." Not your internal jargon.
3. **Look at the ads.** Read every ad at the top of the page. Then run five to ten more searches your customer might run.
4. **Write down everything.** Headlines, descriptions, offers, guarantees, numbers, calls to action. All of it, as potential lines for your own.

Congratulations. You just built your own personal swipe file.

Then go one level deeper. Google's free [Ads Transparency Center](https://blog.google/technology/ads/announcing-the-launch-of-the-new-ads-transparency-center/), launched in March 2023, lets you look up an advertiser and see the ads they've run, where those ads ran, and the last date each one ran. Search your three biggest competitors by name. For Facebook and Instagram, Meta's Ad Library does the same job.

## Why are the ads still running the ones that work?

The ads still running are the ones that work because budget kills losers fast. An advertiser who pays for clicks that don't convert either fixes the ad or turns it off. Survival is the proof.

Clicks are not cheap. WordStream by LocaliQ's 2026 benchmark report put the [average cost per click at $5.42](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/what-are-good-google-ads-benchmarks/546995/) across industries, with legal services near $10. Run the math on a blank-page test. Say you want 100 clicks on each of three headlines before you trust a winner. At the average, that's about $1,600 to learn which guess was least wrong. (That's an illustration, not a client number. Your industry will run higher or lower.)

So weight what has persisted. One ad you see once proves little. An angle that shows up across several advertisers, month after month, is the market telling you what it rewards.

## What should you collect besides the headline?

Collect the angle, not just the words. The words belong to your competitor. The patterns belong to the market.

| What to record | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| The lead promise | What buyers care about most ("same-day," "flat rate," "no contract") |
| The offer | What it takes to earn the click (free quote, discount, trial) |
| The proof | What makes the claim believable (reviews, years, licenses, numbers) |
| The objection they answer | What buyers worry about ("no hidden fees," "cancel anytime") |
| The call to action | What next step the market accepts ("Book today," "Get a quote") |

Sort your file by those five rows. Patterns show up fast. If four of five plumbers lead with "upfront pricing," price surprise is the fear in that market. Your ad has to answer it, in your words, with your proof.

## Does the swipe file help the landing page too?

Yes. The promise that wins the click is the promise that should greet the visitor on the page. If the ad says "flat-rate pricing" and the page opens with your company history, you paid for a click and broke the promise in the first second.

This is where most of the money leaks. I've watched one change on a page take a client's conversion rate from about 1.2% to 6.5%, which I wrote up in [The Surgical Tweak](/grimoire/the-surgical-tweak). Same traffic. The swipe file tells you what the market expects to read after the click. Put that line at the top of the page.

## Isn't this just copying your competitors?

No. Copying is lifting an ad word for word. A swipe file is studying what the market rewarded, then writing your own. Every good copywriter already works this way.

Your business is different. The specifics won't transfer. The structure will: which promise earns the click, how the offer gets framed, which objection gets handled before the buyer raises it. Take the structure. Supply your own substance.

And if a competitor's ads look bad? Check how long they've run. Bad ads don't last on a paid platform. If one survives for months, it's working for a reason you haven't spotted yet. Find the reason before you dismiss it.

## What should you do before your next campaign?

Spend twenty minutes collecting before you spend a dollar testing. Run ten customer searches. Pull your top three competitors in the Ads Transparency Center. Sort everything into promise, offer, proof, objection, and call to action. Then write your first ads from the angles that survived, and make sure the landing page keeps the promise the ad made.

Add to the file every time you launch. Simple and skipped is where the easy edge lives. Don't reinvent. Collect.
