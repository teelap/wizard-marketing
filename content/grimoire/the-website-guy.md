Small business owners want one thing from a website: to say what they need and have it handled.

I learned that by being the website guy. I started Brio Studio in 2018, sold it in 2019, and started Wizard Marketing right after. For the next six years it was an SEO and web shop for smaller clients, the kind of job where you write the strategy, the copy and the backend on the same project because nobody else will. I sold it in 2025. Seven years, counting Brio.

In those seven years, the owners who called me wanted to stop thinking about their website.

## What do small business owners want from a website?

They want the site to be right every time a customer looks at it. Current hours. Current prices. Current photos. A page for the service they started offering last month. They don't want a design process. They want a short line from "can you change this" to done.

I've said it on TikTok in plain words: "As a small business owner, you don't have time to learn everything there is to know about web design." My job was to do the web design so they never had to learn it.

In Eight Dominoes terms, that's the [Core Attraction](/grimoire/core-attraction): the feeling a customer is paying for. For a website, that feeling is getting their attention back.

## Why do small website changes take so long?

The change is small, but the process around it has five steps. One new opening hour turns into an email, a spot in someone's queue, a quote or an invoice, a draft to check, and a reply to approve it. Every step waits on a person.

Owners have three ways to handle a website, and each one costs something:

| Option | What the owner gets | What it costs them |
|---|---|---|
| Build it yourself | The tools, plus all of the work | Your own time, every time something changes |
| Pay a person | Someone else's hands | $100 to $149 an hour at web design firms on Clutch, plus an email, an invoice and a wait for every change |
| Go without | Nothing to manage | Customers who can't check you out. 17% of small businesses have no website, and 25% of firms with fewer than 10 employees |

Sources: [Clutch web design pricing](https://clutch.co/web-designers/pricing), [Clutch's 2025 small business website survey](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250821848152/en/Clutch-Report-No-Code-Tools-Fuel-Website-Growth-Yet-17-of-Small-Businesses-are-Still-Offline), and the [NFIB 2025 Small Business and Technology Survey](https://www.nfib.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2025-NFIB-Technology-Survey.pdf).

Once a small change takes a week, owners stop asking. The site goes stale. I've admitted this one on camera too: "sometimes we build websites and then we just kind of forget about them."

## Why don't website builders fix it?

Website builders hand owners the tools and the work. That's a fair trade if you like building. Most of the owners I worked with didn't, and the tools aren't getting simpler. In a Wix versus WordPress breakdown I said it straight: "There are so many menus and options in Wix that it can be confusing to know where to go." With a builder, every change after launch stays on the owner's list.

## What made clients stay with their website guy?

Clients stayed for direct access. As a one-man shop, I had turnaround times longer than what the bigger agencies promised, and clients stayed anyway. Client after client told me some version of the same thing: they loved getting me on the phone or by email instead of a junior account manager.

Then I hired a team, and the clients who had been with me longest started getting restless. Some left. The work was still good. What they had bought, a direct line to the person doing the work, was gone.

The same pattern shows up before anyone signs. I once went back through 100 of my sales calls. In more than two-thirds of them, the prospect told me they'd had a bad experience with a marketing agency before. Most owners have already tried paying someone. They want someone who answers.

## What should you ask before you hire a website person?

Ask about the process before the portfolio. A beautiful site that takes a week per change will be out of date within a year. Four questions settle it:

1. How long does a one-line change take, from my request to live?
2. Who do I talk to when I need something?
3. Do I see the change before it goes live, and can I undo it?
4. What does a small change cost, and when do I find out?

If the answers are "a week," "a ticket system," "no" and "on the invoice," you're buying a design project, and you'll be back to emailing for every change.
