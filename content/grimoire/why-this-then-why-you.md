Every sale answers two questions, in order.

Why this: do I even need this kind of thing at all. Why you: of all the options, why yours.

Almost every business pours its budget into the second one. Comparison pages, feature grids, testimonials, "we're different because." Then the pipeline comes in thin and the blame lands on the leads, or the channel, or the market being soft. The market is not soft. You are arguing about brands of umbrella with someone who does not believe it is going to rain.

## Why does "why you" only work on people who already answered "why this"?

Because by the time anyone runs the comparison, most of the deciding is already done.

6sense surveyed more than 4,000 buyers across North America, EMEA and APAC for its 2025 Buyer Experience Report, published November 2025. 94% of buying groups had ranked their preferred vendors before the first conversation with any seller. They bought from that pre-contact favorite 77% of the time. The balance between independent research and seller engagement moved from a 70/30 split to 60/40.

Read those in order. The comparison you built all your copy for happens in a room you are not in. Your "why you" shows up to confirm a decision, not to create one. If your name was not already in that room when the ranking started, the best comparison page in your category is a rebuttal filed after the verdict.

## How small is the crowd actually comparing right now?

Small. Small enough that it should change where the money goes.

Professor John Dawes at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute put a number on it and LinkedIn's B2B Institute made it famous. At any given moment roughly 5% of your potential buyers are in the market. The other 95% are out of it. Not uninterested. Just not now.

The number comes from how rarely categories get bought at all:

| Category | Typical cycle | Share of buyers on that cycle |
|---|---|---|
| Business computers | Once every 4 years | 75% of companies |
| Banking services | Once every 5 years | 80% of companies |
| New cars | Once every 10 years | 90% of consumers |

A four-year cycle means about one buyer in sixteen is shopping in any given quarter. You wrote your feature grid for that one. Everything you spend on it lands on the smallest, most contested, most expensive audience you have.

## What is "why this," exactly?

"Why this" is a demand question. "Why you" is a positioning question. They are not two flavors of the same work, and they do not compete for the same budget.

Why this makes someone believe the problem is real, expensive, and worth acting on now. Notice what is missing from that sentence. You are. Why this has nothing to do with your product, and it grows the number of people who are in the market at all.

Why you is where your mechanism, your promise, and your proof go to work. It is necessary. It is second. And it can never be bigger than the demand built ahead of it.

Ehrenberg-Bass calls the triggers category entry points, the situations that send a person looking. In their reading advertising does not push anyone to buy. It builds memory links so a brand surfaces when one of those situations arrives. Why this attaches you to the situation. Why you wins the moment it shows up.

## Why your leads feel so expensive

Because you are only ever talking to the 5%, and so is every competitor you have.

Skip why this and you buy every lead at the point of maximum competition, when the buyer already holds a shortlist and your job is to unseat somebody on it. That is the most expensive yard on the field. It also resets. The why-you fight starts from zero every quarter, because nothing you spent on it compounds into next quarter's demand.

I built 110K+ followers on TikTok without pitching a service. The videos that worked explained how DSP advertising actually works, why one local business shows up in the map pack and the one next door does not, what a T-shaped marketer is and why it makes you hard to replace. All why this. None of it was why me. It went from [400 followers to six figures](/grimoire/tiktok-growth-case-study), and the work it fed came from people who had already decided the problem was real, in my words, and knew whose name to bring up.

Counted over time, that is [share of brand voice](/grimoire/share-of-brand-voice): how often you are the name already in the room. It is also the reason the [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes) run in a fixed order. The first move sets what every move after it is allowed to accomplish, and running the later ones harder never repairs a missing first one.

## The falsifiable version

Stated so it can be wrong. Take two competitors in the same category with the same budget. One spends the year on comparison assets. The other spends most of it making the case for the problem itself and keeps the comparison assets lean. Eighteen months out, the second should hold a larger share of first-contact shortlists and a lower cost per closed deal. If the comparison-only competitor wins on both, I am wrong and why this is a luxury.

Two honest discounts on the sources. Ehrenberg-Bass says 95% is a heuristic and not a measurement, and that the true split moves with your category's purchase cycle. A category bought monthly has a far fatter in-market slice than one bought every five years, so run your own math before you budget against the headline. And 6sense sells buyer-intent data, so a report concluding that influence happens before sellers arrive is a report that sells 6sense. Discount it on that basis. The direction survives the discount, because the Ehrenberg-Bass work reaches the same place by a completely different method.

## Sort your last ten pieces

Pull up the last ten things your marketing produced and put each one in a column. Why this, or why you.

If nine of them land in the why-you column, you have been talking to the 5% and calling it a strategy. The fix is not louder positioning. It is spending real budget on the case for the problem, so that when someone finally starts ranking vendors, you are not making an argument.

You are already on the list.

No spell here. Answer both. In that order.
