In early July 2026, Sony confirmed new PlayStation games go disc-free from 2028. Domino's UK posted a joke the same day. Then it posted a [fake corporate press release](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/dominos-mocks-sony-digital-shift-1806449) announcing it would stop making real pizzas and switch to "digital pizzas" you download and enjoy "using the power of the imagination." That one did 6.2 million views, 305,000 likes, and 41,000 reposts. KFC piled on with PNG chicken. No media spend. No campaign.

The obvious read is be funny like Domino's. That read misses the machine underneath. The funny is downstream of the setup, and the setup is a staffing decision most brands are too scared to make.

## Why can't a committee do this?

Because approval kills the two things that make social work. Speed dies, and cultural fluency dies with it.

Speed is the first casualty. The window on a cultural moment is hours, not a sprint cycle. Domino's landed its joke the day Sony announced and its follow-up the next day. Route that through legal, then brand, then a VP who wants one word changed, and you ship on Thursday to a room that moved on Tuesday. The moment does not wait for your process.

Cultural fluency is the quieter casualty. You cannot brief in the instinct for what a room is feeling. The person who nailed the PlayStation joke understood exactly why fans were mad because they were near enough to hear it, not because a strategy deck told them. A committee flattens that instinct into something safe, and safe is the same as invisible. Sequence is the problem, not effort. Same idea that runs the [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes): the right move made in the wrong order topples nothing.

## What is the actual staffing model?

One to three people, with total carte blanche. That is the whole thing.

Not a team of ten with a shared calendar and a shared fear. A small crew with taste and the keys, trusted to talk like a human in public without clearing it first. Wendy's. Duolingo. Domino's. Different categories, same shape. The account that feels alive is almost always a couple of people who were handed real permission and told to use it.

This is why copying the output never works. Brands watch Wendy's and try to reverse-engineer the tone, so they schedule a snarky post and it lands like a dad at a skate park. The tone was never the asset. The asset was two or three people who understood the room and were trusted to react inside it. You are trying to buy the joke when the thing for sale is the trust that made the joke possible.

## Isn't handing the brand to a small crew reckless?

The reckless move is the invisible account. Handing the keys to a trusted crew is the safe one, and it only looks backward because most orgs measure the wrong risk.

A bad post gets deleted by lunch and forgotten by Friday. A brand nobody remembers does not get a second turn at the mic. Pick the risk you can actually survive, and it is not the occasional miss. It is the years of beige, approved, on-brand nothing that trains an audience to scroll past you on sight.

The unhinged accounts people quote at each other are not the product of a loosened tone guide. They are a trust job. Wendy's works because a very small group is trusted to be that sharp, and trust is the load-bearing beam, not the voice. Legal still matters. Give it a lane, not the wheel. Its job is to keep you out of court, not to keep you out of the conversation. This is the social version of a rule I keep coming back to: [don't outsource the handshake](/grimoire/dont-outsource-the-handshake). The human moment is the whole thing, and you do not hand it to a process that lives at the median.

## Does this only work for pizza chains?

No. The register changes, the shape holds.

Every category has a room. B2B has one, full of people trading takes in comment sections and group chats. Local business has one, the town's running conversation about what is good and who to trust. The volume and the jokes change, but the move is identical: be a recognized voice inside the conversation instead of a banner floating above your own vertical. A landscaper cannot run Wendy's account. A landscaper can be the name that shows up with a real opinion when the neighborhood is talking about the drought.

The failure mode is worth naming, because it looks like the win. Brands that pile onto every trend without a stance get eaten, because everyone can tell they are visiting. You drop into a moment, say the safe version of what everyone else is saying, and add nothing. The room clocks it instantly. The fix is not more trend-chasing. It is living there before you need anything, so that when the moment comes, the account that speaks is already a regular. Attention is the whole game, and being in the room is how you earn it before you spend it. ## Where does this leave you?

Asking a better question. Not "how do we get funnier on social," but "who has the keys, and do we actually trust them."

If the answer is a committee and a five-step approval flow, the account is going to keep missing the room, and no amount of budget fixes a timing problem. If the answer is a small crew you would bet the voice on, the moments start finding you, because you were already there when they showed up.

Hire for taste. Hand over the keys. Then get out of the way. The account is not a megaphone. It is a seat at the table, and empty seats do not get remembered.
