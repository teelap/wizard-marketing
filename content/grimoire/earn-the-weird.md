Every "best campaigns of 2026" list is stacked with the same kind of work. Heinz redesigned its fry box for the first time in 75 years so it actually holds ketchup. Coke turned its contour bottle into a chopstick. The Ordinary [built fake grocery stores and shelved a $175 banana](https://www.famouscampaigns.com/2026/05/the-ordinary-opens-fake-grocery-store-puts-175-banana-on-sale/) to mock beauty markups. Argos [parked a Smeg fridge inside a giant toy box](https://www.famouscampaigns.com/2026/06/argos-puts-a-smeg-fridge-in-a-giant-toy-box-to-settle-the-toy-shop-debate/), stamped "Ages: 25+." Famous Campaigns [crowned the lot](https://www.famouscampaigns.com/2026/06/the-best-brand-campaigns-of-2026-so-far/) and took a shot at brands that "turn up with millions of media spend and blitz our feeds."

The obvious read is be clever, not loud. That read is half right and dangerous. Weird is not how you earn attention. Weird is what you earn with it. Reverse that order and the fridge in the toy box lands on nobody.

## Why isn't the stunt the strategy?

Because the stunt spends attention it did not create. Coke turns its bottle into a chopstick because the whole planet already knows the bottle. The joke runs on borrowed recognition. Pull the same move before anyone knows your name and there is no recognition to borrow. You are telling an inside joke to a room that never got the setup.

This is why "make a thing, not an ad" keeps failing for the businesses that copy it. They see the reward and mistake it for the method. The weird work on that list is the victory lap of brands that already own their category's attention. It is not the race. Order matters here the way it matters everywhere in marketing. Sequence is the problem, not effort. Same instinct that runs the [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes): a step out of order does not just underperform, it topples nothing.

## Do small businesses need Coke's budget for this?

No. They need depth, not budget. That is the part the big-spend takedown gets right and then buries. The entry fee for weird is not scale. It is a group of people who already pay close attention to you.

Attention does not have to be massive. It has to be deep. A hundred people who care more than the average customer will travel, share, and come back to see what you did next. That is enough permission to be strange. A million strangers scrolling past is not. Depth beats size, and depth is the one thing a landscaper, a distillery, or a dentist can actually build. You measure it the way you measure any real audience: branded search, repeat visits, the regulars who bring a friend. If you want the metric, it is the same one behind [Share of Brand Voice](/grimoire/share-of-brand-voice). Deep attention shows up as people looking for you by name.

## What does earning the weird actually look like?

It looks like a reason to come back that keeps changing. I ran a distillery, Copper Fiddle, that was barred from selling beer or wine. So we built strange, creative, one-off cocktails, and rotated them. People came in to find out what the new weird thing was this week. The weird was the product. The experience was the reason to return.

That is the move, and it is smaller than a fake grocery store. We did not have a media budget. We had a bar full of regulars and a standing promise that the strange thing would be different next time. The reframe stops being "how do we go viral" and becomes "what can we make that people want to experience again and again." Repeatable beats one-off. The new thing on the menu beats the single stunt nobody remembers by Friday.

## Aren't "be everywhere" and "be weird" opposites?

No. They are simpatico, and they run in order. Being everywhere earns the attention. The weird is what you do with it once people are looking.

You go everywhere first because nobody can buy you if they do not know you exist. That is the whole job when you are growing. Then the experience does the second job. It deepens the attention you bought, and it gives people a reason to look again. The everywhere brings them in. The weird brings them back. Read the campaign list again with that in mind and it stops being an argument against big reach. Those brands earned the room with years of it. The stunt is what they spent it on.

## Where does this leave you?

Asking a better question. Not "what is our viral stunt," but "have we earned one yet." If nobody is looking, the work is attention, not theater. Show up everywhere. Get one channel right, then the next. Build the small deep group that checks on you on purpose. Only then does the strange thing land instead of echoing in an empty room.

And when you have earned it, do not waste it on merch for merch's sake. A drop with a logo and no idea under it is a costume. The weird has to create something worth repeating, or it is a one-off nobody remembers. The brands on that list earned their weird. The move for everyone else is to earn theirs, one deep fan at a time.

No trick to it. Earn the room first. Then earn the strange.
