## Improvement makes buyers compare. Replacement makes them convert.

Stop trying to be a better version of the market leader. The moment you say "20% cheaper than the big guys," the big guys become the ruler you get measured with. A new opportunity says the old way is broken and hands the buyer a different vehicle to the same destination. Improvement triggers evaluation. Replacement triggers excitement.

This is Domino 08, New Opportunity, the last piece of the [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes). Everything before it builds toward one moment: the customer realizes you aren't a better option inside their category. You're a different category.

## Why "better" is a death sentence

Positioning as an improvement accepts the customer's existing frame, and the market leader built that frame. You can't out-incumbent the incumbent. They have the budget, the shelf space, and the years of memory in the buyer's head.

So "better" sends the buyer straight into comparison mode. Feature grids. Price checks. "Let me get a few quotes." Every one of those behaviors favors the known name. The research has a word for it: status quo bias. Samuelson and Zeckhauser documented in 1988 that people stick with the default option far more often than the math says they should. An improvement pitch asks the buyer to leave a safe default for a slightly better unknown. Most won't.

A replacement pitch asks a different question. Not "is this better than what I have?" but "why am I still doing it the old way?"

## Dollar Shave Club didn't build a better razor

Dollar Shave Club is the cleanest proof of replacement I know, because the product was not the point. The vehicle was.

Gillette owned the razor aisle. Blades sat behind locked plastic, priced like jewelry. Michael Dubin didn't try to out-engineer that. He changed how you buy a razor: a dollar a month, shipped to your door. In March 2012 he posted a launch video that cost about $4,500. It crashed the site in the first hour and pulled 12,000 orders in 48 hours.

| | Improve the old way | Replace the old way |
|---|---|---|
| The pitch | "A sharper blade, a few bucks cheaper" | "Stop paying for the plastic case. A dollar a month, at your door." |
| Reference point | Gillette | None |
| Buyer behavior | Compares in the aisle | Signs up |

By 2015, Slice Intelligence data put Dollar Shave Club at [roughly half the online razor market](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/how-a-humorous-social-media-spot-sparked-a-razor-war/444902/), with Gillette around 21%. In 2016 [Unilever bought it for a reported $1 billion](https://techcrunch.com/2016/07/19/unilever-buys-dollar-shave-club-for-reported-1b-value/), and the prize was its 3.2 million members.

Unilever didn't pay a billion dollars for razor blades. They paid for a position in 3.2 million minds.

## Attack the paradigm, not the people

Depositioning makes the old way look outdated without trash-talking anyone. Take whatever you're uniquely best at and make it the game. Marty Marion put a name to this for me, and the seat-belt line is the one I keep: if you're the only car with seat belts, you don't say "our cars are safer." You ask, "shouldn't a car protect the people inside it?"

That question doesn't name a competitor. It names an assumption. Everyone still operating on the old assumption, including you if you'd stayed, suddenly looks like they missed something fundamental.

The test I run on every positioning line:

1. **Does it name the old way?** If the buyer can't picture what they're leaving, they won't leave.
2. **Does the old way feel broken, or a little behind?** A little behind is still an improvement.
3. **Is the new way about their outcome?** If the line brags about your innovation, rewrite it around their liberation.

## Replacement works for service businesses, not only startups

You don't need a new invention to replace a category. You need a new frame for the work you already do well.

When I ran SEO, the category default was the static annual plan: map out twelve months, execute, report. I didn't invent a new science. I took the same work, reorganized it into weekly sprints, and positioned it against the static calendar. Same craft. Different vehicle. Once prospects saw SEO run in sprints, the yearly plan looked slow, and they couldn't unsee it.

A landscaping client did the same thing on a bigger stage. They stopped competing with other landscapers on mowing and mulch and started selling outdoor-living transformation. Nobody could line them up against the crew down the street anymore, because nobody else was selling that. It's the same landscaper whose numbers I cite most: 3 crews to 9, $1.2 million to over $10 million in revenue.

Neither story needed a patent. Both needed the courage to stop playing the incumbent's game.

## The customer should feel found, not sold

Done right, a new opportunity reads like a gift. The customer thinks "where has this been my whole life," not "let me weigh the features." If they can see the seams, you're still selling. If the seams disappear, they're converting.

That's why this domino comes last. [Core Attraction](/grimoire/core-attraction) found the emotion. The hook, the promise, the stakes, the mechanism, the objections, and the limitation each knocked down one more reason to hesitate. The new opportunity is where the whole chain lands in one option that doesn't compete. It replaces. Read [how the dominoes fall in sequence](/grimoire/the-domino-effect) if you want the full run.

## Find the assumption your category treats as law

Write down the one thing everyone in your industry treats as a law of nature. The annual contract. The locked case. The quote-and-wait. That assumption is your opening.

Then read your homepage headline and ask one question: does it make the old way feel broken, or only slightly worse? If it's slightly worse, you're still competing on the leader's terms, and the leader wins those. Don't improve the old way. Replace it.
