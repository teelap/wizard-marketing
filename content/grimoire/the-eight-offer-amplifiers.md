Most offers get described when they should get framed. An offer amplifier is one of eight angles that tells a buyer why your product matters: New, Only, Big, Fast, Easy, Safe, Predictable, Simple. Pick the one or two that are true for you and lead with them.

I open this lesson on TikTok with two lines. Your product is probably fine. Your messaging probably sucks. I've spent 13 years in marketing and built an agency that crossed seven figures, and the amplifiers are the cheapest fix I teach. The product stays the same. You change which true thing you say first.

## What are the eight offer amplifiers?

The eight offer amplifiers are New, Only, Big, Fast, Easy, Safe, Predictable, and Simple. Each one frames a product around a single reason to care.

| Amplifier | What the buyer hears | Words that carry it |
|---|---|---|
| New | This is what's hot | discover, unveiling, introducing |
| Only | I can't get this elsewhere | exclusive access, patented |
| Big | The result is large | massive, grand, colossal |
| Fast | The result comes soon | instant, rapid, accelerated |
| Easy | I avoid the work | effortless, user-friendly |
| Safe | I won't regret this | rest easy, low-risk, trust |
| Predictable | No surprises | consistent expectations |
| Simple | I won't be confused | direct, streamlined, straightforward |

Direct-response copywriters have run shorter versions of this list for years. Kyle Milligan's is four words: New, Easy, Safe, Big. I teach eight because Only, Fast, Predictable, and Simple each answer a hesitation the first four miss. These eight are a copy tool. They sit on top of the messaging sequence in [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes), and they don't replace it.

## Why does describing an offer fail?

A description fails because it makes the buyer do the framing, and the buyer has about ten seconds. Jakob Nielsen's [write-up of Microsoft Research dwell-time data](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/) found visitors often leave a web page in 10 to 20 seconds, and the first 10 decide most of it. His conclusion: communicate your value proposition inside 10 seconds or lose the visit.

"We make project management software" is honest and forgettable. It names a category and stops. Take a made-up example. "We offer financial planning" describes a service. "Predictable retirement income, no surprises" leads with an amplifier. Same service. The second line tells the buyer which problem you solve.

Framing can feel like spin. You're choosing which true thing to say first, and that choice is the job. The amplifier is the language layer on the [sixty-second promise](/grimoire/the-sixty-second-promise).

## How many amplifiers should one offer use?

Use one or two. An offer that claims all eight claims none of them.

The reason is choice overload. In Iyengar and Lepper's jam study, a table of 24 jams drew more browsers, and a table of 6 sold about ten times better. Reasons to buy behave like jams. Hand a stranger six and they carry none of them out of the room.

Picking two forces a decision you've been avoiding: what are you best at? You can't lead with everything, so you have to rank. You came for a copywriting trick and got a positioning exercise.

## Which amplifier should you lead with?

Lead with the amplifier that answers your buyer's fear. Skip the one you happen to like.

Kahneman and Tversky measured losses hurting about twice as much as equal gains. Buyers weigh what could go wrong before they weigh what could go right, so the winning frame speaks to the risk on their mind.

- **A nervous buyer** wants Safe and Predictable.
- **A busy buyer** wants Fast and Easy.
- **A status buyer** wants Only.

Most hesitation hides behind a polite excuse. I covered how to find the real one in [The Objection Is Never the Objection](/grimoire/the-objection-is-never-the-objection). Find it first. Then choose the amplifier that speaks to it.

## What do amplifiers look like in famous ads?

Famous lines lead with one or two amplifiers and drop the rest. These are my reads of three of them.

1. **FedEx, "When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight."** Fast and Predictable. Price, reach, and service never make the sentence.
2. **Domino's, "30 minutes or it's free."** Fast, with a guarantee that made it Predictable. The pizza's taste goes unmentioned.
3. **Apple's iPod, "1,000 songs in your pocket."** Big and Simple. The spec sheet said 5GB, and nobody repeated that at dinner.

Each company had more it could have claimed. Each one chose.

## Aren't amplifiers just hype adjectives?

An amplifier becomes hype the moment it's false. A true one reads as clarity.

"Fast" dropped into a sentence as filler is noise. "Fast" chosen because speed is the thing your buyer is desperate for is a strategy. Same word. A different decision sits behind it.

A false or reckless amplifier costs you. Domino's retired the 30-minute guarantee in 1993 after liability suits. The promise worked for years, and then the company couldn't keep it safely. Claim what you can deliver on your worst week.

## How would this be proven wrong?

Test it. Run two headlines on the same page with the same traffic. One describes the offer. The other leads with a single true amplifier matched to the buyer's fear. Then run a third that stacks five amplifiers. If the description or the stack beats the single frame on conversions across several offers, I'm wrong and the choosing doesn't matter. I expect the single frame to win and the stack to lose to both.

## Run your headline through all eight

Open your homepage and read the first line out loud. Ask which of the eight it runs on. If the answer is none, you've found why it reads flat.

Now say the offer eight ways, one per amplifier. Two will sound true. Six will sound like a stretch. Keep the two, put the stronger one first, and rewrite the line around it. Then show it to a stranger and count the seconds until they can tell you what you're for.
