Pay-for-performance is being sold as the fair model. It is not. It is a devaluation model, and the whole market is sliding toward it at once.

HubSpot now charges $0.50 for a [resolved conversation](https://martech.org/hubspot-moves-to-outcome-based-pricing-for-some-breeze-ai-agents/) instead of a flat fee on its Breeze customer agent, live since April 14. Target [killed its commission creator program](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/why-target-killed-its-creator-program-launched-2-new-ones/) and replaced it with Club Target, where the commission only arrives once you have earned your way up the tiers. McKinsey ties [about a quarter of its global fees](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/mckinsey-ties-25-of-fees-to-outcomes-as-ai-erodes-billable-hours) to client outcomes now. Performance-based creator deals hit [53% of brand partnerships in 2026](https://www.affiliatebooster.com/performance-based-creator-deals/), up from 23% two years ago. Same move at every tier of the market. Earn on results, not on work.

It sounds like accountability. We only win when you win. Skin in the game. Read the fine print. Fairness shares risk. This transfers it. It prices your expertise at zero until you have already made someone money, then hands you a slice of a funnel you own one piece of. That is not a partnership. It is a demotion with a nicer name.

## What is pay-for-performance actually doing to your price?

It removes the floor. A fair deal puts a real fee under the work and a bonus on the result. Pay-for-performance deletes the fee and keeps only the maybe. Your skill, your judgment, your twenty years, all priced at zero until proven on this one specific deal.

I watched this from the inside. I ran an agency past seven figures, and in the last year, the clients who left did not leave over performance. They left with smaller budgets, or they pulled the work in-house to run it with AI. The market did not decide our work was bad. It decided it could not carry the old price. Pay-for-performance is what that decision looks like written onto a contract.

The tell is who is moving which way. Brands are sprinting toward these deals. The people expected to carry them are walking. Both sides are rational. They sit on opposite ends of the risk.

## Why are brands racing toward it while the providers walk?

Because a scared economy buys down its own risk, and the provider is the cheapest place to park it. The economy is not booming. It is propped up by AI bets, and everything downstream is soft. Travel, e-commerce, consulting, all tighter. People have less to spend. Businesses cannot hold the numbers they held two years ago, so the only lever left is lowering the buyer's risk. Pay-for-performance is that lever.

For the person doing the work, the math runs the other way. Take one of these deals unprepared and you work for months on the potential of money. You eat the churn. You carry the risk for a result the client's own sales team, landing page, and pricing all touch. The creators saying no are not scared. They cannot float a year of unpaid work for a maybe, and that is a cash-flow decision, not a confidence one.

## Does running it in-house with AI actually cost less?

No, and the work comes out worse. That is the part the spreadsheet misses. When a client pulls marketing in-house to save money and hands it to a generalist plus a chatbot, two things happen. They do not save much. And the output drops.

Getting real quality out of AI takes prompting, taste, version control, creative direction, and iteration most in-house teams do not have. What comes out is the center of the bell curve, shipped faster. I made this case from the buyer's side in [the average is lying](/grimoire/the-average-is-lying), and it holds here too. The machine hands you the middle. The middle is exactly what no customer pays a premium for.

## How do you lower the buyer's risk without pricing yourself at zero?

Make the value obvious and the door cheap, then protect your time with leverage. You can reduce what a buyer stands to lose without deleting your own fee to do it.

The lazy version is to sign the zero-until-you-win deal and hope you are the exception. The better version keeps you paid for the work while still lowering the buyer's downside. Drop the entry point. Keep the delivered value well above the price. Then serve many at once so your own time cost stays low. A room of people paying a small price for value worth far more beats one client paying nothing until a funnel you half-control finally clicks. That is why I moved toward group consulting instead of one-to-one. Same risk reduction the buyer wanted, without selling my worth to nothing to give it. Making the value obvious is a messaging problem before it is a pricing one, which is the whole point of the [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes).

## Where does this leave you?

The question is no longer whether to offer pay-for-performance. It is whether you can float the bet and overdeliver enough to win it.

One kind of operator wins here. The one who can carry six to twelve months of unpaid work and then blow the result out of the water. For that person, performance pays more than a fee ever would, and the upside is real. For everyone else, it is a slow markdown of what they do. So know which one you are before you sign. If the answer is no, do not sign anyway. Lower the buyer's risk another way. A cheaper door, higher delivered value, a one-to-many model that guards your time.

There is no spell here. The market is trying to price your work at zero until it is safe. Lower the risk without lowering your worth, and keep getting paid for the work.
