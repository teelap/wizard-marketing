## The logo is the last decision, not the first

Never design your logo until you've done three things: written your brand doctrine, your brand messaging, and your brand positioning. Most businesses do the opposite. They start a rebrand by hiring someone to draw a picture, because a picture feels like progress. You can show it to your spouse. You can put it on a hat.

That's the last step dressed up as the first. A logo is an expression of a brand. It is not the brand. Start with the picture and you've bought an expensive shape sitting on top of nothing.

I say this in my videos more than almost anything else about branding, because the instinct is so strong and so backwards.

## Why starting with the logo fails

Starting with the logo fails because a logo can only express decisions that already exist. With no decisions underneath, every design review turns into a taste argument. Do we like blue. Is the font too friendly. Nobody can win, because nobody wrote down what the thing is supposed to say.

Gap ran this experiment in public. On October 6, 2010, it swapped its 20-year-old blue box for a Helvetica wordmark with a small gradient square. The backlash hit within 48 hours, and [Gap reverted to the old logo on October 12](https://www.npr.org/2010/10/08/130419187/the-gap-gets-backlash-for-logo-makeover). Six days. The new mark didn't express anything customers believed about Gap, so it read as a costume.

Now the other direction. In 1971, Phil Knight paid a Portland State design student named Carolyn Davidson [$35 for the swoosh](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/09/05/heres-how-much-nikes-billionaire-founder-paid-for-its-swoosh-logo.html). His review: "I don't love it, but maybe it will grow on me." It grew on him because of what Nike built underneath. The shape didn't carry the brand. The brand carried the shape.

## Step one: brand doctrine is what you believe

Brand doctrine is the handful of convictions that drive every decision inside your business. Apple and innovation. Nike and empowerment. Coca-Cola and togetherness. Each one fits in a word, and each one settles arguments before they start.

When you know your doctrine, every other decision in your business gets easier. Hiring, pricing, which client to fire, which product to kill. You finally have a ruler to hold things against.

A quick test for yours: can you name a profitable thing you'd refuse to do because it breaks the belief? If not, you have a slogan, not a doctrine.

## Step two: brand messaging is how the belief reaches people

Brand messaging is how you communicate your doctrine so the market feels it. It isn't only words. It's emotion, tone, even color. Messaging is the doctrine made legible to people who will never read your mission statement.

This is where branding turns into marketing. Branding isn't marketing. But marketing comes out of branding. With the doctrine and the messaging set, campaigns stop being invented fresh every quarter. They come out of a point of view you already hold. ## Step three: brand positioning is where you can and can't compete

Brand positioning is an honest map of the ground you can win and the ground you should never set foot on. Positioning gives your doctrine edges, and edges are what make a brand sharp instead of generic.

Most small businesses skip the "can't" half. They want to be for everyone, so they end up memorable to no one. Write down three buyers you'd turn away. That list tells the market more about you than your tagline does.

## How the three steps make the logo easy

Combine doctrine, messaging, and positioning and you become unignorable in the marketplace. Together they're your brand identity, and that identity is what the logo is supposed to express.

| Question | Logo-first | Brand-first |
|---|---|---|
| What the design review argues about | Taste | Fit |
| What the logo has to say | Undefined | The doctrine |
| How long the logo lasts | Until the next rebrand | Until the doctrine changes |
| Where campaigns come from | New vibes each quarter | The same point of view |

With the foundation set, the logo stops being a debate and becomes a checklist. Does it express the doctrine. Does it carry the messaging. Does it hold the position. Three yeses and you're done.

## The pushback, answered

"We need a logo now to look legitimate." You need a clear point of view to look legitimate. A polished logo on a confused brand reads as a costume, which is exactly what Gap learned.

"This sounds like expensive agency theater." It's three plain documents you could draft this week. They cost thought, not budget.

"Big brands change logos all the time." They change the expression because the foundation underneath is solid. They're updating the picture, not discovering who they are. You're trying to skip straight to the picture.

"I already have a logo I like." Keep it, and run it against the three documents. If it doesn't fit, the logo isn't wrong. It's early.

## Write the three documents before you brief a designer

If your last branding project started with a logo, you started at the end. Open a blank page and write three headings: what we believe, how we say it, where we compete. One paragraph each. Then brief the designer, and hand them those three paragraphs instead of a mood board.

The same logic runs through the [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes): every hook, promise, and offer downstream depends on knowing who you are. Do the three. The logo is the easy part after that.
