You are not the hero of your customer's story. You're the sword they swing.

Most marketing gets the casting wrong. The brand stands in the spotlight with its awards, its journey, its founder photo. The customer sits in the audience. Then everyone wonders why the page doesn't convert.

## Why does marketing that makes the brand the hero fail?

It fails because people act inside stories where they're the hero, and they only watch stories where they aren't. A brand-as-hero page asks the customer to admire you. Admiration isn't the goal. Action is.

Look at a typical "About Us" page. "We were founded in 2009. We pride ourselves on quality. We have a passionate team. We deliver excellence." That's four sentences and zero mention of the person reading. They came with a problem. You handed them a biography.

This is the most common copy mistake I see, by a mile. In 13 years of writing and fixing marketing, the pattern barely changes. Owners are proud of what they built, so they lead with it. Fair enough. But pride is a reason to write the page, not a reason to buy from it.

## What are the four parts of a customer story?

Every story your marketing tells has four parts: a hero, a dragon, a reward, and a sword. Assign each one before you write a word.

| Story part | Who or what it is | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Hero | Your customer | Who is this for? |
| Dragon | Their problem, named honestly | What's in their way? |
| Reward | The life they want once the problem is gone | Why does winning matter? |
| Sword | Your product or service | What do they use to win? |

If you can't fill in all four, you don't have a story yet. You have a description. Descriptions get skimmed. Stories get finished.

The dragon deserves the most work. A vague dragon makes a weak story. "Marketing challenges" is not a dragon. "You're paying for ads every month and can't tell which ones bring in customers" is a dragon. The sharper you name the problem, the more the hero feels seen, and the more they want the sword that was clearly made for that exact fight.

## Why is the product the sword and not the savior?

The product is the sword because the sword doesn't slay the dragon. The hero does, holding the sword. Your job is to be the best weapon for a fight the customer is already in, then get out of the way and let them win.

I think of the brand as the item vendor in town. You're the shop they visit before they head out to face the dragon. They do the fighting. They feel the win. Nobody tells the story of the blacksmith. They tell the story of the knight. And every knight remembers where they got the blade.

This is where I part ways a little with Donald Miller's [StoryBrand framework](https://storybrand.com/), which most marketers have met by now. Miller also makes the customer the hero, and he casts the brand as the guide, the Yoda figure with a plan. That's a big improvement over brand-as-hero. My push is to go one size smaller. A guide still gets lines and a backstory. A sword gets used. The less of the scene you take up, the more of it the customer can step into.

## How do you rewrite your copy so the customer is the hero?

Run every line through the four parts and turn it outward. Each sentence about your greatness becomes a sentence about their fight. A made-up landscaping company shows the move.

**Before (brand as hero):**
> We are an award-winning landscaping company with 15 years of experience. Our skilled team takes pride in delivering beautiful results for every client.

**After (customer as hero):**
> Your yard should be the place you want to spend Saturday, not the chore that eats it. We design it, plant it, and keep it looking right, so the weekends go back to you.

Same company. Same service. The first version is about the shop. The second names the dragon (a yard that eats your weekend), shows the reward (Saturdays back), and puts the service where it belongs: in the customer's hand.

A quick way to check any page:

1. Count the sentences that start with "we," "our," or your company name.
2. Count the sentences that start with "you" or "your."
3. If "we" wins, rewrite until "you" does.

The emotion matters as much as the grammar. The dragon is the feeling they already have when they land on the page. That's the whole idea behind [Core Attraction](/grimoire/core-attraction): name the emotion before you sell the fix.

## Doesn't my brand story matter?

Your brand story matters as proof, not as the plot. Your origin, your awards, and your years in business show that you forge a good sword. They earn trust. They don't earn the lead role.

Customers do want to know who they're buying from. Trust is the shopkeeper being credible, with clear prices, real reviews, and a name on the door. Hijacking the quest is a different thing. Be the vendor they trust, not the protagonist they're asked to cheer for.

And if your product really is impressive, make the sword legendary. Just make it legendary because of what it lets the hero do. To you, the impressive part is the product. To the customer, the impressive part is who they become after the dragon is dead.

This is also why the frame sits inside how I teach messaging in [The Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes). Messaging that's about the business tends to stall everything after it. Messaging that's about the customer gives the rest of the sequence something to push.

## Where this leaves you

Open your homepage. Count the "we" sentences and the "you" sentences. If "we" wins, your customer is watching your story instead of starring in theirs.

Then fill in the four parts. Name the hero. Name the dragon, specifically. Picture the reward. Make your product the sword they reach for.

There is no spell here. Hand them the sword. Let them win the fight.
