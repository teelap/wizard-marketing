Anyone can get likes, hearts, shares, views, and clicks. Content that doesn't convert is noise.

Most marketing dashboards are graded on the easy test. Grade yours on three numbers tied to money instead: opportunities, cost per lead, and return per channel. If the needle didn't move, there was no value, no matter how good the engagement chart looked.

## Why are likes a bad way to measure marketing?

Likes are a bad measure because they're the cheapest signal there is. Anyone can get them. A joke gets them. A cute dog gets them. None of that tells you whether a single person bought.

That cheapness is exactly why likes get reported. They go up and to the right. They're easy to grow. They make the Monday meeting feel good. The easy number and the true number are rarely the same number, and the easy one wins the slide deck every time.

This isn't new. When Duke's CMO Survey asked in August 2013, [only 15% of top marketers could show quantitative proof](https://cmosurvey.org/does-pressure-to-prove-the-value-of-marketing-help-or-hurt-company-performance/) of what their social media spend returned. Twelve years later, [Sprout Social's 2025 survey of 1,200 marketing leaders](https://media.sproutsocial.com/uploads/2025/08/Sprout-Social-2025-Impact-of-Social-Media-Report.pdf) found the same gap: leaders believe social drives revenue, and they say their teams can't prove it. The dashboard filled the silence with likes.

## Why is content that doesn't convert just noise?

Content that doesn't convert is noise because attention you can't spend isn't a win. A viral post that sells nothing is noise with good production values. I'd rather get one conversion than a thousand clicks.

I learned this on my own account. I grew [110,000 followers on TikTok](/grimoire/tiktok-growth-case-study), and the follower count was never the point. The point was that the account turned into over 10 leads a week for my agency. Those leads made the followers worth something. Without them, 110,000 is applause.

Chase engagement and you optimize for engagement. That's a trap. Engagement that doesn't convert is an expensive way to feel busy.

## What should you track instead of vanity metrics?

Track the numbers that survive the question "did the needle move." There are three, and they stack.

| Metric | What it answers | Why likes can't answer it |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunities | How many real chances to sell did we create? | A like isn't a conversation |
| Cost per lead vs. lifetime value | Did each chance cost less than a customer is worth? | Likes have no price tag on either side |
| ROEMI per channel | Which channels returned more than they cost? | Applause doesn't show up in gross profit |

**Opportunities first.** A booked call, a quote request, a demo, a real inquiry. If marketing doesn't create these, nothing downstream matters.

**Then cost per lead against lifetime value.** What did each of those chances cost, compared to what a customer is worth over the whole relationship? My rule of thumb: healthy usually lands somewhere in the single-digit to low-double-digit percent range. If you're paying a big share of a customer's lifetime value just to get a lead, the channel is eating the profit.

## How do you calculate ROEMI?

ROEMI is return on marketing investment, and it's the one number that grades the whole machine. The formula:

**(Gross profit − marketing cost) ÷ marketing cost × 100**

Run it per channel, not across the whole budget. Blended, a winner hides a loser. Split out, you see which channel pays and which one only claps.

A hypothetical, to show the math:

1. **Channel A** spends $2,000 and produces $8,000 in gross profit. ($8,000 − $2,000) ÷ $2,000 × 100 = **300%**.
2. **Channel B** spends $3,000 and produces $2,400 in gross profit. ($2,400 − $3,000) ÷ $3,000 × 100 = **−20%**.

Blended, that's $5,000 in, $10,400 back, and everybody celebrates. Split out, Channel B is losing money. Maybe Channel B is also the one with the best engagement chart. That happens all the time. Pour into A. Fix or cut B.

Is ROEMI just ROI with a different name? Close. It's ROI aimed at marketing spend and run per channel. The name matters less than the habit: grade the spend by what comes back, not by what gets liked.

## Doesn't brand awareness matter, even if it doesn't convert?

Brand awareness matters when you build it on purpose and check whether it ever turns into sales. The problem isn't awareness. The problem is mistaking a like for awareness, and awareness for revenue.

Some of the best marketing is hard to track, and brand effects lag. Fine. "Hard to track" is not the same as "don't bother." Track what you can to revenue and be honest about the rest. If you want to measure awareness properly, measure [Share of Brand Voice](/grimoire/share-of-brand-voice), not the heart count.

And if your boss wants follower growth in the report, give it to them. Put it next to opportunities and cost per lead. If followers climb and money doesn't, the gap makes the argument for you.

## Where this leaves you

Open your marketing dashboard and circle every number tied to money. If most of the screen is impressions, likes, and followers, you've been grading yourself on the easy test.

Add the hard numbers. Opportunities. Cost per lead. ROEMI per channel. Then make next month's decisions off those and nothing else.

There is no spell here. Count what pays. Fund what pays. Cut what claps.
