The marketer who survives every market shift is shaped like a T. The top of the T is broad: you understand everything marketing can do. The stem is deep: you are great at one thing. Breadth makes you useful in any room. Depth makes you the one they can't replace.

I've spent 13 years in marketing and built an agency that crossed seven figures selling SEO, websites, and paid ads. The T is one of the lessons I teach on TikTok, because it answers two questions at once. A marketer asks how to build a career that lasts. A founder asks who to hire. Same letter, both times.

## What is a T-shaped marketer?

A T-shaped marketer knows the whole field at a working level and is excellent at one part of it. The top of the T is context. The stem is the career.

The idea is older than marketing. McKinsey used "T-shaped" inside the firm in the 1980s to recruit consultants, and IDEO's Tim Brown later used it to build design teams, according to the term's [documented history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-shaped_skills). It fits marketing better than most fields, because marketing has more moving parts than one person can master.

| Shape | What they know | What happens to them |
|---|---|---|
| Flat generalist | A little about everything | First to be automated, outsourced, or passed over |
| Narrow specialist | Everything about one thing | Builds strong work that doesn't fit the plan |
| T-shaped | The whole field, plus one deep skill | Gets hired for the stem, kept for the range |

## Why do generalists get replaced first?

A generalist who is great at nothing is the easiest person to replace, because the shallow version of every marketing task is now cheap. A first-draft email, a starter keyword list, a basic report: software produces each of them in seconds.

The hiring market already shows it. The American Marketing Association's [2026 State of Marketing Careers Report](https://ama.org/marketing-news/2026-career-report), a survey of 1,412 marketers, found the share of marketing job postings mentioning AI doubled in 2025. The report calls AI fluency "a baseline expectation, not a differentiator." Knowing a bit about everything used to be a job. Now it is the minimum.

AI returns the most likely answer, and the most likely answer is the average. I wrote about that in [The Center Point](/grimoire/the-center-point). If average work across eight skills is your whole offer, you are bidding against a tool anyone can open in a browser tab.

## Why do pure specialists break the strategy?

A specialist who can't see past their lane improves their own piece while the plan around it fails. The best SEO in the world who doesn't understand conversion sends traffic to a page that leaks.

I've seen how much money sits one step outside the lane. One change to a page took a client's conversion rate from about 1.2% to 6.5%. Same traffic. Same rankings. An SEO who only reads ranking reports never finds that fix, because the problem sits one step outside the job title.

Marketing runs in sequence. That is the whole argument of [Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes): each step sets up the next one. Great at one step and blind to the other seven is a liability with a skill.

## Doesn't AI make the broad layer pointless to learn?

No. Breadth is how you point the tools and judge what comes back. A deep skill with no context has no idea where to aim.

The same AMA report sorts marketing skills by how hard they are to automate. Marketing strategy and critical thinking land in the least-disrupted, human-led group. Yet marketers in that survey rated critical thinking as less important in 2026 than they did in 2025. The field is undervaluing the exact skill the tools can't supply. Learn the broad layer as judgment. Stop treating it as a list of tasks.

## Which deep skill should you pick?

Pick the skill you'd still find interesting after a thousand reps that the market already pays for. Interest survives the grind. Demand pays the bills.

Run three checks before you commit:

1. **Would you do it on a bad day?** Depth takes years. Mild curiosity won't last.
2. **Does someone pay for it now?** Search the job boards and freelance rates. Real budgets, not predictions.
3. **Does it connect to revenue?** A deep skill that ends in a sale is safer than one that ends in a view count.

Then stay put. It is the same discipline as [one good channel](/grimoire/one-good-channel): finish one thing before you start the second.

## How do founders use the T to hire?

Hire T's for every seat: deep enough to own the function, broad enough to plug into the rest of the team. A team of narrow specialists doesn't add up to a strategy.

Two interview questions do most of the work. Ask the candidate to show you the one thing they do best, with proof. Then ask what happens to the customer right before and right after their part. The first answer measures the stem. The second measures the top. Someone who can only answer one of them is a flat line, and a flat line costs you more than the salary.

## How would this be proven wrong?

Stated so it can fail. Track marketing roles over the next three years. If generalists with no deep skill hold their jobs and pay as well as marketers who pair range with one clear specialty, I'm wrong and the stem is optional. I expect the opposite: the flat generalist goes first, the specialist with no range goes second, and the T keeps the seat.

## Draw your own T

Draw it on paper and be honest. Is the top wide enough that you could explain how every channel feeds the next? Is the stem deep enough that someone would hire you for that one skill by name?

If you're all top, pick the stem this week. If you're all stem, read one level outside your lane every month until you can see where your work lands. Know everything you can do. Be great at one of them.
