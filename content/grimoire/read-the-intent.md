Name the intent behind a keyword before you write a word. The searcher wants a specific site, an answer, a comparison, or a way to buy. Build the page for that job, or no amount of optimization will rank it.

I started my agency building websites for landscapers. Thirteen years in marketing later, the SEO failure I see most often has nothing to do with links, speed, or word count. A business owner builds a good page, aims it at the wrong intent, and then spends months tuning a page that never had a chance.

## What is search intent?

Search intent is the job a person wants done when they type a query. Google's whole objective is to hand the searcher value as fast as possible, so it ranks the page that does that job fastest. The keyword tells you the topic. The intent tells you what kind of page wins.

"Best CRM" and "buy CRM" share a topic. They want different pages. Google knows the difference even when the person writing the page doesn't.

## What are the four types of search intent?

The four types are navigational, informational, commercial, and transactional. Once you know them, you can look at any keyword and know how to write for it.

| Intent | What the searcher wants | Example search | Page to build |
|---|---|---|---|
| Navigational | A specific site or page | "jake the wizard grimoire" | Your homepage or the exact page, easy to find |
| Informational | To learn something | "how to aerate a lawn" | A guide or how-to |
| Commercial | To compare before buying | "best landscaper near me" | A comparison, reviews, proof |
| Transactional | To act now | "lawn aeration quote" | A service page with price and a booking path |

Google's own raters sort queries a little differently. The September 2025 [Search Quality Rater Guidelines](https://guidelines.raterhub.com/searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf) use Know, Do, Website, and Visit-in-Person. Know matches informational. Website matches navigational. Do covers buying and downloading. Visit-in-Person is the one local businesses should circle. Google treats "find a place near me" as its own job, and that is why a local search shows a map pack before it shows your site.

## Why does a good page fail to rank?

A good page fails when it answers a question nobody asked. Picture a landscaper (an illustration, not a client) who writes a polished sales page and aims it at "how to aerate a lawn." The people typing that want to do it themselves this weekend. Google fills page one with guides and videos, and the sales page never shows up.

The page isn't bad. It's pointed at the wrong job. More keywords won't fix it, and neither will a longer page. The fix is a different page: a real how-to guide for the do-it-yourself searcher, plus a separate service page aimed at "lawn aeration service" for the person who wants it done for them.

The everything-page loses for the same reason. A page that teaches, compares, and sells at once serves no intent cleanly. A focused competitor matches the searcher faster, and Google rewards the faster match.

## How do you find the intent of a keyword?

Search it and read what already ranks. Google has decided the dominant intent, and page one shows you the decision. It takes about 30 seconds per keyword.

1. **Search the keyword** in a private window so your history doesn't skew the results.
2. **Look at the first five organic results.** Guides mean informational. Product or service pages mean transactional. "Best of" lists and review sites mean commercial.
3. **Check what Google puts above the links.** A map pack signals local, visit-in-person intent. An AI Overview signals a question Google thinks it can answer on the spot.
4. **Copy the format, then beat it.** If page one is ten guides, you need a guide. Make yours more specific, more current, and built from something only you know.

The commercial and transactional line blurs the most. "Best landscaper near me" is a buyer still shopping. "Landscaper quote" is a buyer ready to call. The first wants proof that you're the right pick. The second wants a phone number and a price.

## Does search intent still matter with AI Overviews?

Search intent matters more now, because AI Overviews took over the bottom of the informational funnel first. Semrush tracked more than 10 million keywords and found that in January 2025, [91.3% of searches that triggered an AI Overview were informational](https://www.semrush.com/blog/semrush-ai-overviews-study/). By October 2025 that share had dropped to 57.1%. Commercial searches grew to 18.57% of AI Overview triggers and transactional searches to 13.94%.

Read that two ways. First, a page built to explain a basic fact now competes with Google's own answer, so it earns fewer clicks. Write those pages to be the source the AI quotes, with real numbers and first-hand detail. I covered that side in [The Most-Mentioned Wins](/grimoire/the-most-mentioned-wins). Second, AI is moving down the funnel toward buyers. Your comparison and service pages need to be clear enough for a machine to summarize and specific enough for a buyer to choose you.

The intent still decides the page. AI changed who reads it first.

## What should you do this week?

Take your five most important keywords and label each one with its intent. Then open the page you built for each keyword and ask one question: does this page do the job the searcher came to do?

Every mismatch is a page that will never rank, however hard you optimize it. Rebuild those first. If paid search covers the same keywords, line the ads up to the same intents. I break down how the two work together in [SEO and PPC Together](/grimoire/seo-and-ppc-together).

Five keywords, five labels, one afternoon. Start with the page that brings in the most traffic and the fewest calls.
