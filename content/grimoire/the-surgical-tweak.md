You probably don't need more traffic. You need the traffic you already have to convert.

One button change took a client's page from about 1.2% to 6.5%. Same visitors. Five times the conversions. No new ads, no new keywords, no redesign. Fix the page before you buy another click, because more traffic to a leaky page just buys you a bigger leak.

## Why is more traffic usually the wrong fix?

More traffic is the wrong fix because it multiplies whatever the page already does, including the leak. When sales slow down, the reflex is to buy reach: more ads, more SEO, more posts. The numbers at the top go up, so it feels like growth. The sales at the bottom barely move.

Run the math on your best page. At a 1.2% conversion rate, 10,000 visitors give you 120 customers. Double the traffic and you get 240, and you paid for 10,000 extra visitors to get them. Double the conversion rate instead and you get 240 from the visitors you already had.

| Move | Visitors | Rate | Customers | New spend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | 10,000 | 1.2% | 120 | None |
| Double the traffic | 20,000 | 1.2% | 240 | 10,000 more visitors |
| Double the rate | 10,000 | 2.4% | 240 | The fix |
| The button story | 10,000 | 6.5% | 650 | The fix |

The visitor counts are illustrative. The rates in the last row are the real client result. A conversion win also compounds. Every campaign you run after the fix pays out at the new rate.

## What happened with the 1.2% to 6.5% button?

A single change to one button lifted a client's page from about 1.2% to 6.5%. That's a five-times lift on the same traffic. It wasn't a redesign, a rebrand, or a new funnel. It was a precise fix to the element that mattered.

Buttons aren't magic. The highest-leverage fixes are small, specific, and tested. Big redesigns change twenty things at once, so when the numbers move you can't tell why. A surgical tweak changes one thing, so you learn something either way.

Other teams have found the same pattern at scale. UX researcher Jared Spool [wrote about](https://articles.centercentre.com/three_hund_million_button/) a retailer that replaced a forced "Register" button with "Continue" and let people buy as guests. Purchases rose 45%, worth $300 million in the first year. One button again. The fix was removing a step, not adding traffic.

## How do people actually read your page?

People don't read your page. They scan it. [Nielsen Norman Group documented the F-shaped pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/) back in 2006: across the top, a shorter sweep below that, then straight down the left edge. They still call it the default pattern when a page gives the eye no strong cues.

So the F is a fallback. People default to it when your page is a wall of text. Give them headings, bold the words that carry meaning, and put the call to action where the eye already goes. If the hot spots on your page hold a logo, a mission statement, and a stock photo, you've decorated the places that matter most.

## Why does talking about yourself kill conversions?

Talking about yourself kills conversions because the visitor came for their problem, not your history. The most common conversion leak I see is a page that's all "we." We were founded. We believe. We're passionate. The customer scrolls past all of it looking for one answer: what's in this for me?

Write the page about them. Make the customer the hero and your offer the tool that helps them win. Open on a promise they care about, the way [a sixty-second promise](/grimoire/the-sixty-second-promise) opens a pitch. Then put the contact button everywhere, so nobody ever has to wonder how to buy.

## What is the click budget?

The click budget is the small amount of effort a visitor will spend on you before they leave. Every extra click, field, or step draws it down. [Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate) puts average cart abandonment at 70.22% across 50 studies, and 18% of shoppers who quit checkout name being forced to create an account. That's a step, and it costs real sales.

The best landing pages I've built are a headline, an image, and a form. Every element you remove is one less place for the visitor to stall. Subtract until what's left is the conversion.

## How do you find the one thing to change?

Watch what people do, then change the one element they stall on. Pull up a heatmap or session recordings on your highest-traffic page. Look for where people stop scrolling, where they hover and don't click, and where they leave. The tweak comes from behavior, not from taste.

1. **Pick the page.** Your highest-traffic page with a clear action on it.
2. **Find the stall.** The spot where visitors drop off or hesitate.
3. **Change one element.** The button, the headline, a form field, a step.
4. **Test it.** Run the old and new versions against each other, then keep the winner.

Your designer saying the page looks great isn't the test. Looking great and converting are different jobs. This is the same logic behind [the Eight Dominoes](/eight-dominoes): fix the piece that's blocking everything behind it, in order, before you push harder on the front.

## Where this leaves you

Ask one question about your best page. If twice as many visitors converted on it tomorrow, would you still need more traffic?

Usually the answer is no. I'd rather get one conversion than a thousand clicks. A thousand clicks that don't convert are an expensive way to feel busy.

There's no spell here. Find the leak. Change one thing. Test it. Then buy the traffic.
